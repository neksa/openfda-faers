Adverse event reporting in FAERS, 2004–2026

A reproducible analysis of the complete FDA Adverse Event Reporting System corpus

Author

Alexander Goncearenco

Published

September 3, 2026

Abstract

This report analyses every adverse event report the FDA has published since 2004 — 20.3 million after deduplication, spanning both the legacy LAERS and current FAERS eras. It rebuilds and corrects an earlier exploratory analysis that worked from a 10,000-report sample drawn through the rate-limited openFDA API, and extends it to the questions that sample could not support: signal detection with shrinkage and multiplicity control, confounder adjustment, and the internal structure of a report. Every number is produced by a DVC pipeline from FDA’s own published archives.

What this is, and what it replaces

An adverse event report is a voluntary notification that something undesirable happened to someone taking a medical product. It names the patient’s drugs and the reactions observed. It does not say which drug caused which reaction — that link is simply not recorded — and no report is validated before it enters the database.

Everything below follows from that. These data can show that an event is reported disproportionately often alongside a drug. They cannot show that the drug caused it, and they cannot estimate how often it happens, because the number of people taking the drug without incident is unknown. Disproportionate reporting is a signal to investigate, not a finding.

This analysis replaces a 2020 Jupyter notebook that explored the same database through the openFDA web API. That notebook asked good questions and was candid about its limits: it could not compute reactions per report, could not deduplicate, could not correct for the confounders it had itself identified, and drew its record-level conclusions from a 10,000-report sample. The API made all four impossible. Working instead from FDA’s quarterly ASCII archives — 3.5 GB covering the whole corpus, against 113 GB for the equivalent JSON — removes all four constraints.

NoteProvenance

90 quarterly archives, 3,547,347,845 bytes, 2004Q1–2026Q2, each pinned by SHA-256. 24,399,723 raw report records parsed into a canonical schema spanning both the LAERS (2004Q1–2012Q3) and FAERS (2012Q4–) eras.

How much of the database is actually distinct

A case is resubmitted every time follow-up information arrives, and each submission appears in a different quarterly file. Counting submissions therefore overcounts patients. Separately, FDA publishes lists of cases it has withdrawn.

16.7% of the raw corpus is redundant. The 2020 notebook saw the consequence of this without being able to name it: a block of “quite repetitive” rows in its clustergram, which it guessed came from “duplicates in the database or other artifacts”. It did — 4.0 million superseded versions of cases counted again each time they were resubmitted.

82,134 cases were opened under LAERS and followed up under FAERS. These only link because case identifiers survived the 2012Q4 transition; an analysis treating the two eras as separate datasets would double-count every one of them.

A second duplicate class exists: the same incident submitted independently by, say, a patient and their physician, under different case ids. Those cannot be collapsed by identifier because they share none. They are estimated separately by record linkage — see Independent duplicates below — and are not removed from the figures here.

The corpus, and who is in it

Reports grew roughly sevenfold, from 205,154 in 2004 to a peak near 1.57 million in 2021. 2026 is partial. Growth of this size over 22 years is itself a confounder: a drug approved in 2022 is evaluated against a baseline dominated by reports that predate it, which is why calendar period is adjusted for later.

Among reports recording a sex, 60.3% are female — matching the notebook’s earlier estimate. The more consequential number is what is missing: 41% of reports record no usable patient age at all. Any age-stratified conclusion rests on the 59% that do, and that subset is not a random sample.

Consumers now file more reports than physicians — 8.88 million against 4.46 million. This is a genuine change in the character of the database rather than a processing artifact, and it cuts against the intuition that FAERS is primarily a clinician-reported system. Consumer reports carry less clinical detail, which is part of why the missing-age fraction is so high.

What is reported

Acetaminophen has displaced aspirin at the top since 2020. The leading reactions are worth reading carefully: DRUG INEFFECTIVE (1.27M) and OFF LABEL USE (839k) outrank every clinical event. Neither is an adverse reaction — they are reasons a report was filed. DEATH ranks third, and is an outcome whose cause is unstated. The notebook flagged exactly this and it remains true: the most common terms in the reaction field are substantially not reactions.

The single most common indication, by a factor of six, is PRODUCT USED FOR UNKNOWN INDICATION (5.43M). Stratifying by indication — the obvious way to control for why a drug was prescribed — is therefore unavailable for a quarter of the corpus.

The structure of a report

The notebook could not compute this. It noted the API “does not allow analytics other than counting reports with only one variable to group by”, and concluded the data would have to be loaded into Spark or a relational database first.

A report carries a mean of 3.18 drugs and 2.98 reactions, but a median of one drug — the mean is pulled up by a long tail reaching 171 drugs and 456 reactions on a single report. That tail is why the drug–event join has to be built carefully: the worst single report contributes 39,648 drug–event pairs on its own, and the corpus generates 126,512,749 pairs in total.

This structure is also the reason no drug can be attributed a reaction. With three drugs and three reactions on a report, all nine combinations are equally supported by the record.

Dechallenge and rechallenge

Withdrawing a drug and seeing the reaction abate — then reintroducing it and seeing the reaction return — is the strongest causal evidence a spontaneous report can carry. The notebook tried to quantify how much of the database has it, estimated roughly 2% from marginal counts, and closed the section noting “more data preprocessing needed”.

Computed directly from the joint distribution, with missing values kept distinct from negative outcomes:

0.147%, not 2%. The earlier estimate was inflated by reading marginal counts as if the three fields were independent; they are not. The practical consequence is stark: for roughly 1 drug record in 680 does the database contain the validation protocol that would support a causal reading. The rest is association.

Which drug–event pairs report disproportionately

Every measure below is computed on a 2×2 table of reports, restricted to primary and secondary suspect drugs, for pairs with at least 3 co-reports — 1,997,725 pairs over 20,320,042 reports.

Four screening rules are applied. All four are reported because none is authoritative, and their disagreement is informative.

Each rule screens on a lower confidence or credible bound, never a point estimate. This matters more than it sounds: a reporting odds ratio of 8 computed from two co-reports is noise, and screening on the raw ratio is the most common way to manufacture spurious signals. The 2020 notebook used the lower bound for ROR and PRR, which was right, but evaluated it on two hand-picked pairs.

EB05 is the most conservative at 685,128 pairs, and is very nearly a subset of the others: 685,128 pairs satisfy all four rules simultaneously. That is the shrinkage doing its job.

The gamma-Poisson mixture converged from multiple starting points. Points fall below the diagonal precisely where evidence is thin — an extreme raw ratio from a handful of reports is pulled back toward the null, while a ratio supported by thousands of reports is left alone.

Does it recover what we already know?

A signal-detection pipeline that cannot rediscover established drug safety findings is not trustworthy on novel ones. Nine associations that appear on approved product labelling, several as boxed warnings, were checked blind against the output:

All nine are flagged by every rule. Implausible pairings behave correctly in the other direction: aspirin–alopecia returns EB05 = 0.19 and is not flagged.

What ranks highest, and why that is a warning

The pairs with the largest EB05 in the whole corpus are not adverse reactions:

A radioisotope tracer is administered in order to perform the scan whose abnormality is recorded. Anosognosia — a patient’s unawareness of their own illness — is a feature of the psychosis zuclopenthixol treats, not a reaction to it. Raised prolactin is the expected pharmacology of a dopamine antagonist. Further down the same ranking, “gadolinium deposition disease” names its own cause. These are indication bias, expected pharmacology and tautology, ranked at the very top by every measure — and they rank there precisely because the association is real and near-deterministic.

This is the practical lesson of the whole exercise. Disproportionality finds strong statistical association. Whether that association reflects harm, the reason for prescribing, or a naming convention is not decidable from these data.

Do signals survive confounder adjustment?

The notebook identified sex and age as likely confounders and could not act on it. Each is applied here as a separate Mantel–Haenszel stratification, along with calendar period, on a random sample of 18,740 signalling pairs.

The result is two-sided, and the second half matters more than the first.

Adjustment removes almost nothing — 98.5% to 100% of signals survive. Confounding by sex, age or period is not what generates these associations.

But homogeneity is rejected for half to three-quarters of them. A pooled Mantel–Haenszel estimate assumes one common odds ratio across strata; where Breslow–Day rejects that, the pooled number averages genuinely different effects and is misleading on its own. The honest reading is that these confounders do not create signals but do modify them, and a stratum-specific estimate should be read instead of the pooled one.

One caveat stated plainly: at these sample sizes Breslow–Day has enormous power and will detect heterogeneity too small to matter clinically. The test was calibrated against simulated data with a known common odds ratio and showed a 6.8% rejection rate against a nominal 5%, so it is not simply always rejecting — but the effect sizes, not the p-values, are what should drive interpretation.

Reporting artefacts over time

The notebook compared raw yearly counts of Death, Pain and Dyspnoea and read the divergence as a reporting bias. Raw counts rise with the corpus, so that comparison partly measured database growth. Plotted as a share of each year’s reports, the picture is different: what looked like a rising trend in Death is substantially the database getting larger.

Drug names that are not ingredients

The unit of analysis is supposed to be the active ingredient. For a large minority of entries it is not: the reported string was a brand or product name that the resolution cascade could not map, and it survives into the results under its own name.

The consequence is not cosmetic, and it runs in two directions at once. When HUMIRA . PEN is counted separately from ADALIMUMAB, adalimumab’s marginal is understated — the reports filed under the brand never reach it. Meanwhile the brand string carries a small marginal of its own, which inflates its disproportionality: it is being compared against the whole database on a fragment of its true exposure. Both the numerator and the denominator are wrong, in opposite directions.

Two free sources close most of this gap, and the pipeline uses both: FDA’s NDC directory (public domain, 7,958 brand tokens usable after an ambiguity filter) and NLM’s RxNav. Together with stricter cleaning of device and product designators, they consolidate brands into their substances — DURAGESIC-100 into fentanyl, ADVAIR DISKUS 100/50 into fluticasone and salmeterol, PARAGARD 380A into copper. The fallback-only count fell from 3,838 to 2,008.

A second split ran alongside the brand one and was easy to miss because both halves looked like real substances. FAERS records ATORVASTATIN and ATORVASTATIN CALCIUM, and counting them separately divided one drug almost exactly in half — 275,333 reports against 269,328. The same happened to warfarin, amlodipine and metformin. Salt forms are now resolved to their active moiety through RxNorm, which gets the hard cases right without a heuristic: SODIUM CHLORIDE and CALCIUM CARBONATE are returned unchanged, where stripping a suffix would have produced “sodium” and “calcium”.

NoteReading the percentage above

The fallback share rose when salts were merged, from 23.5% to the figure in the table, while the fallback count fell slightly. Merging affected curated ingredients — the ones RxNorm knows — far more than unmapped free text, so the denominator shrank faster than the numerator. The absolute problem got smaller; the ratio did not.

The effect on real signals is visible: atorvastatin–rhabdomyolysis rose from 2,397 co-reports (EB05 = 15.5) to 2,794 (EB05 = 28.6) once Lipitor’s reports joined the substance they belong to. The signal was always there; part of its evidence was filed under another name.

A residue remains and always will. Discontinued brands appear in neither reference, and foreign and misspelled products appear in nothing at all. Those entries keep their reported name and are marked ingredient_curated = false in the published table, so they can be filtered rather than mistaken for substances.

One correction to an earlier draft of this report, which claimed these rows fall below the minimum-support threshold and never enter the signal tables: they do enter it, and always did. The claim was inferred from the min_count rule rather than measured, and it was wrong.

Do reaction profiles recover pharmacology?

If two drugs act through the same mechanism, they may provoke the same pattern of reported reactions. The 2020 notebook explored that with UMAP and DBSCAN, described two clusters, and left interpretation as “out of scope” — so whether the clusters meant anything was never established.

The measure here is cosine similarity on information-component profiles. Raw co-report counts would make every drug resemble every other, because both profiles are dominated by the same ubiquitous terms; the IC is normalized against what the corpus predicts, so what remains is what is distinctive about a drug. No embedding step is involved, which also makes the result deterministic rather than a function of projection hyperparameters.

The question is whether it recovers classes it was never told about:

Every class is 3.0–5.8× more similar to itself than the corpus average, without any drug-class information entering the computation. The nearest neighbours are recognisable pharmacology:

Atorvastatin’s neighbours are other statins and ezetimibe — a different mechanism, but the same therapeutic setting and co-prescription pattern. Adalimumab sits beside infliximab and etanercept, then ustekinumab: TNF inhibitors first, then a biologic used for overlapping indications. Sertraline sits among SSRIs, then venlafaxine, an SNRI.

That last detail is the honest caveat. This measure cannot separate shared mechanism from shared indication or shared co-prescription — the same confound that puts indication bias at the top of the disproportionality rankings. It recovers therapeutic neighbourhoods reliably; reading a specific mechanism into any one pair requires evidence from outside these data.

Vocabulary drift, measured

MedDRA is revised twice a year. Terms are added, renamed, split and merged, so a reaction term’s frequency can move for reasons that have nothing to do with patients. Earlier versions of this report declared that as a limitation. It is now measured.

Correcting drift needs the licensed MedDRA release history. Noticing it does not: a term that goes from absent to established between two adjacent quarters, or the reverse, is behaving like a vocabulary change. Real clinical trends do not switch on in one quarter and stay on.

Roughly a quarter of reaction mentions involve a term whose trajectory looks like vocabulary change rather than epidemiology. Any statement about a reaction becoming more or less common over this window has to contend with that. The pooled disproportionality analysis is unaffected — it aggregates over the whole window and never asks when a term appeared — but the temporal figures are, and the per-term flags in results/drift/term_drift.parquet mark which terms to distrust.

This is a floor, not a ceiling. A rename between two similarly-named terms shows up on both sides and is caught; a gradual merge of one term into another may not be visible at all.

Independent duplicates

Deduplication earlier in this report collapsed case versions — follow-up submissions carrying the same case id. It cannot see the other kind of redundancy: one incident reported separately by the patient, the physician and the manufacturer, each becoming a distinct case with its own id. FDA does not link these and no field identifies them.

Detected here by record linkage: block on sex, five-year age band, event year-month, reporter country and a shared drug and a shared reaction, then score candidate pairs on how much their drug and reaction sets overlap.

Two numbers, because they answer different questions. Only 30% of the corpus records a sex, an age, an event date and a reporter country, and without all four a record cannot be blocked at all. Among records that could be examined, 9.1% appear to be redundant. Across the whole corpus the figure is 2.7%, but that denominator mixes “not redundant” with “never examined” — the true rate is somewhere between, and closer to the higher figure if missingness is unrelated to duplication.

These reports have not been removed. Dropping half a million records would move every number in this document, and a matcher with unknown precision should not do that silently. The estimate is published; removal is a parameter (duplicates.remove) that is off by default. Anyone who wants the deduplicated variant can turn it on and re-run.

Limitations

  • No causality, and no rates. These are reporting associations. The denominator — people exposed without an event — is unknown, so incidence cannot be estimated.
  • MedDRA drift. Preferred terms are treated as stable strings across 22 years, but MedDRA has been revised continuously and terms are renamed, split and merged. Some apparent temporal trend is vocabulary change. Correcting this needs the licensed MedDRA release, which cannot ship here.
  • No drug class hierarchy. RxNorm→ATC mapping requires a UMLS licence. Cluster interpretation by pharmacologic class is therefore not attempted rather than approximated badly.
  • A substantial minority of “ingredients” are really product names. See the section below — this is the most consequential limitation of the analysis and it is quantified there rather than summarized here.
  • Approximate q-values. Benjamini–Hochberg assumes independence or positive dependence. Drug–event tests share reports and are dependent, so the stated FDR level is approximate.
  • Independent duplicates are estimated, not removed. See the section above: ~9% of linkable records. Blocking makes that a lower bound, and the reports remain in every table here.
  • Partial dates. Dates given as year or year-month are completed to the start of the period, biasing those toward January and month-starts.

Reproducing this

git clone https://github.com/neksa/openfda-faers && cd openfda-faers
uv sync
dvc repro

No credentials and no cloud storage are needed: FDA hosts the source archives, dvc.lock pins every intermediate by hash, and results/source_manifest.json records a SHA-256 for each of the 90 inputs. FDA republishes quarterly extracts as snapshots and can revise them in place, so faers verify reports drift against those checksums rather than letting it change the numbers silently.

Full run is roughly 25 minutes on a 10-core laptop, dominated by the download.

on the raw ratio is the most common way to manufacture spurious signals. The 2020 notebook used the
lower bound for ROR and PRR, which was right, but evaluated it on two hand-picked pairs.

EB05 is the most conservative at `{python} n(sig["signals"]["signal_eb05"])` pairs, and is very nearly
a subset of the others: `{python} n(sig["concordant_all_four"])` pairs satisfy all four rules
simultaneously. That is the shrinkage doing its job.

```{python}

fig("shrinkage_effect")
```

pl.DataFrame(

The gamma-Poisson mixture converged from multiple starting points. Points fall below the diagonal

### Does it recover what we already know?

A signal-detection pipeline that cannot rediscover established drug safety findings is not
trustworthy on novel ones. Nine associations that appear on approved product labelling, several as
boxed warnings, were checked blind against the output:

### What ranks highest, and why that is a warning

The pairs with the largest EB05 in the whole corpus are **not** adverse reactions:

```{python}
# | label: tbl-top-signals
top = (
    scored.filter(pl.col("n_ij") >= 200)
    .sort("eb05", descending=True)
    .head(6)
    .select("ingredient", "pt", "n_ij", "eb05")
    .collect()
)
pl.DataFrame(
    {
        "Ingredient": [r["ingredient"].title() for r in top.to_dicts()],
        "Reaction term": [r["pt"].capitalize() for r in top.to_dicts()],
        "Co-reports": [n(r["n_ij"]) for r in top.to_dicts()],
        "EB05": [f'{r["eb05"]:,.0f}' for r in top.to_dicts()],
    }
).to_pandas()
```

A radioisotope tracer is *administered in order to* perform the scan whose abnormality is recorded.
Anosognosia — a patient's unawareness of their own illness — is a feature of the psychosis
zuclopenthixol treats, not a reaction to it. Raised prolactin is the expected pharmacology of a
dopamine antagonist. Further down the same ranking, "gadolinium deposition disease" names its own
cause. These are indication bias, expected pharmacology and tautology, ranked at the very top by
every measure — and they rank there precisely *because* the association is real and
near-deterministic.

This is the practical lesson of the whole exercise. Disproportionality finds strong statistical
association. Whether that association reflects harm, the reason for prescribing, or a naming
convention is not decidable from these data.

## Do signals survive confounder adjustment?

The notebook identified sex and age as likely confounders and could not act on it. Each is applied
here as a separate Mantel–Haenszel stratification, along with calendar period, on a random sample of
`{python} n(strat["by_variable"][0]["tested"])` signalling pairs.

```{python}
# | label: tbl-strat
pl.DataFrame(
    {
        "Adjustment": ["Sex", "Age band", "Calendar period (5y)"],
        "Pairs tested": [n(v["tested"]) for v in strat["by_variable"]],
        "Still signalling": [
            f'{n(v["surviving"])} ({v["survival_rate"]:.1%})' for v in strat["by_variable"]
        ],
        "Heterogeneous (Breslow–Day p<0.05)": [
            f'{n(v["heterogeneous"])} ({v["heterogeneous"]/v["tested"]:.0%})'
            for v in strat["by_variable"]
        ],
    }
).to_pandas()
```

The result is two-sided, and the second half matters more than the first.

**Adjustment removes almost nothing** — 98.5% to 100% of signals survive. Confounding by sex, age
or period is not what generates these associations.

**But homogeneity is rejected for half to three-quarters of them.** A pooled Mantel–Haenszel estimate
assumes one common odds ratio across strata; where Breslow–Day rejects that, the pooled number
averages genuinely different effects and is misleading on its own. The honest reading is that these
confounders do not *create* signals but do *modify* them, and a stratum-specific estimate should be
read instead of the pooled one.

One caveat stated plainly: at these sample sizes Breslow–Day has enormous power and will detect
heterogeneity too small to matter clinically. The test was calibrated against simulated data with a
known common odds ratio and showed a 6.8% rejection rate against a nominal 5%, so it is not simply
always rejecting — but the effect sizes, not the p-values, are what should drive interpretation.

## Drug names that are not ingredients

The unit of analysis is supposed to be the active ingredient. For a large minority of entries it
is not: the reported string was a brand or product name that the resolution cascade could not map,
and it survives into the results under its own name.

```{python}
# | label: tbl-unresolved
drug_ing = pl.scan_parquet(ROOT / "data" / "curated" / "drug_ingredients.parquet")
ever_confident = (
    drug_ing.group_by("ingredient")
    .agg((pl.col("resolution") != "unresolved").any().alias("confident"))
)
ing_in_signals = scored.select("ingredient").unique()
joined = ing_in_signals.join(ever_confident, on="ingredient", how="left").collect()

total = joined.height
fallback = joined.filter(~pl.col("confident").fill_null(False)).height

pl.DataFrame(
    {
        "Distinct ingredients in the signal table": [n(total)],
        "Resolved by a curated path": [f"{n(total - fallback)} ({(total - fallback) / total:.1%})"],
        "Free-text fallback only": [f"{n(fallback)} ({fallback / total:.1%})"],
    }
).to_pandas()
```

The consequence is not cosmetic, and it runs in two directions at once. When `HUMIRA . PEN` is
counted separately from `ADALIMUMAB`, adalimumab's marginal is **understated** — the reports filed
under the brand never reach it. Meanwhile the brand string carries a small marginal of its own,
which **inflates** its disproportionality: it is being compared against the whole database on a
fragment of its true exposure. Both the numerator and the denominator are wrong, in opposite
directions.

Two free sources close most of this gap, and the pipeline uses both: FDA's **NDC directory**
(public domain, 7,958 brand tokens usable after an ambiguity filter) and NLM's **RxNav**. Together
with stricter cleaning of device and product designators, they consolidate brands into their
substances — `DURAGESIC-100` into fentanyl, `ADVAIR DISKUS 100/50` into fluticasone and salmeterol,
`PARAGARD 380A` into copper. The fallback-only count fell from 3,838 to 2,008.

A second split ran alongside the brand one and was easy to miss because both halves looked like
real substances. FAERS records `ATORVASTATIN` **and** `ATORVASTATIN CALCIUM`, and counting them
separately divided one drug almost exactly in half — 275,333 reports against 269,328. The same
happened to warfarin, amlodipine and metformin. Salt forms are now resolved to their active moiety
through RxNorm, which gets the hard cases right without a heuristic: `SODIUM CHLORIDE` and
`CALCIUM CARBONATE` are returned unchanged, where stripping a suffix would have produced "sodium"
and "calcium".

::: {.callout-note}
## Reading the percentage above
The fallback *share* rose when salts were merged, from 23.5% to the figure in the table, while the
fallback *count* fell slightly. Merging affected curated ingredients — the ones RxNorm knows — far
more than unmapped free text, so the denominator shrank faster than the numerator. The absolute
problem got smaller; the ratio did not.
:::

The effect on real signals is visible: atorvastatin–rhabdomyolysis rose from 2,397 co-reports
(EB05 = 15.5) to 2,794 (EB05 = 28.6) once Lipitor's reports joined the substance they belong to.
The signal was always there; part of its evidence was filed under another name.

A residue remains and always will. Discontinued brands appear in neither reference, and foreign and
misspelled products appear in nothing at all. Those entries keep their reported name and are marked
`ingredient_curated = false` in the published table, so they can be filtered rather than mistaken
for substances.

One correction to an earlier draft of this report, which claimed these rows fall below the
minimum-support threshold and never enter the signal tables: they do enter it, and always did. The
claim was inferred from the `min_count` rule rather than measured, and it was wrong.

## Do reaction profiles recover pharmacology?

If two drugs act through the same mechanism, they may provoke the same pattern of reported
reactions. The 2020 notebook explored that with UMAP and DBSCAN, described two clusters, and left
interpretation as "out of scope" — so whether the clusters meant anything was never established.

The measure here is cosine similarity on **information-component** profiles. Raw co-report counts
would make every drug resemble every other, because both profiles are dominated by the same
ubiquitous terms; the IC is normalized against what the corpus predicts, so what remains is what is
*distinctive* about a drug. No embedding step is involved, which also makes the result
deterministic rather than a function of projection hyperparameters.

The question is whether it recovers classes it was never told about:

```{python}
# | label: tbl-similarity
simsum = load_json("similarity_summary.json")
labels = {
    "statins": "Statins",
    "ace_inhibitors": "ACE inhibitors",
    "tnf_inhibitors": "TNF inhibitors",
    "ssris": "SSRIs",
    "bisphosphonates": "Bisphosphonates",
}
rows = [
    {
        "Drug class": labels.get(k, k),
        "Members": v["members"],
        "Within-class similarity": v["within_class_similarity"],
        "Vs. corpus average": f'{v["vs_overall"]}×',
    }
    for k, v in simsum["validation"].items()
]
pl.DataFrame(rows).to_pandas()
```

**Every class is 3.0–5.8× more similar to itself than the corpus average**, without any drug-class
information entering the computation. The nearest neighbours are recognisable pharmacology:

```{python}

    "ATORVASTATIN CALCIUM",
    "LISINOPRIL",
    "ADALIMUMAB",
    "SERTRALINE HYDROCHLORIDE",
]

for probe in probes:
    top = nn.filter(pl.col("ingredient") == probe).sort("rank").head(3)
    if top.height:
        rows.append(
            {
                "Drug": probe.title(),
                "Nearest by reaction profile": ", ".join(
                    f'{r["neighbour"].title()} ({r["cosine"]:.2f})' for r in top.to_dicts()
                ),
            }
        )
pl.DataFrame(rows).to_pandas()
```

Atorvastatin's neighbours are other statins **and ezetimibe** — a different mechanism, but the same
therapeutic setting and co-prescription pattern. Adalimumab sits beside infliximab and etanercept,
then ustekinumab: TNF inhibitors first, then a biologic used for overlapping indications. Sertraline
sits among SSRIs, then venlafaxine, an SNRI.

That last detail is the honest caveat. This measure cannot separate *shared mechanism* from *shared
indication* or *shared co-prescription* — the same confound that puts indication bias at the top of
the disproportionality rankings. It recovers therapeutic neighbourhoods reliably; reading a specific
mechanism into any one pair requires evidence from outside these data.

## Vocabulary drift, measured

MedDRA is revised twice a year. Terms are added, renamed, split and merged, so a reaction term's
frequency can move for reasons that have nothing to do with patients. Earlier versions of this
report declared that as a limitation. It is now measured.

Correcting drift needs the licensed MedDRA release history. *Noticing* it does not: a term that
goes from absent to established between two adjacent quarters, or the reverse, is behaving like a
vocabulary change. Real clinical trends do not switch on in one quarter and stay on.

```{python}
# | label: tbl-drift
drift = load_json("drift_summary.json")
pl.DataFrame(
    {
        "Measure": [
            "Terms tested (>= 200 reports)",
            "Flagged as drift-suspect",
            "  - absent early, established later",
            "  - established early, absent later",
            "  - abrupt step between adjacent quarters",
            "Share of reaction mentions involving a flagged term",
        ],
        "Value": [
            n(drift["terms_tested"]),
            f'{n(drift["terms_drift_suspect"])} ({drift["terms_drift_suspect_fraction"]:.1%})',
            n(drift["late_onset"]),
            n(drift["discontinued"]),
            n(drift["step_change"]),
            f'{drift["report_mentions_affected_fraction"]:.1%}',
        ],
    }
).to_pandas()
```

**Roughly a quarter of reaction mentions involve a term whose trajectory looks like vocabulary
change rather than epidemiology.** Any statement about a reaction becoming more or less common over
this window has to contend with that. The pooled disproportionality analysis is unaffected — it
aggregates over the whole window and never asks when a term appeared — but the temporal figures
are, and the per-term flags in `results/drift/term_drift.parquet` mark which terms to distrust.

This is a floor, not a ceiling. A rename between two similarly-named terms shows up on both sides
and is caught; a gradual merge of one term into another may not be visible at all.

## Independent duplicates

Deduplication earlier in this report collapsed case *versions* — follow-up submissions carrying the
same case id. It cannot see the other kind of redundancy: one incident reported separately by the
patient, the physician and the manufacturer, each becoming a distinct case with its own id. FDA does
not link these and no field identifies them.

Detected here by record linkage: block on sex, five-year age band, event year-month, reporter
country **and** a shared drug **and** a shared reaction, then score candidate pairs on how much
their drug and reaction sets overlap.

```{python}
# | label: tbl-duplicates
dup = load_json("duplicates_summary.json")
pl.DataFrame(
    {
        "Measure": [
            "Records eligible for linkage",
            "Candidate pairs compared",
            "Pairs above similarity threshold",
            "Suspected duplicate clusters",
            "Redundant records (all but one per cluster)",
            "As a share of eligible records",
            "As a share of the whole corpus",
        ],
        "Value": [
            f'{n(dup["eligible_records"])} ({dup["eligible_fraction_of_corpus"]:.1%} of corpus)',
            n(dup["candidate_pairs"]),
            n(dup["pairs_above_threshold"]),
            n(dup["clusters"]),
            n(dup["redundant_records"]),
            f'{dup["redundant_fraction_of_eligible"]:.1%}',
            f'{dup["redundant_fraction_of_corpus"]:.1%}',
        ],
    }
).to_pandas()
```

Two numbers, because they answer different questions. Only **30%** of the corpus records a sex, an
age, an event date *and* a reporter country, and without all four a record cannot be blocked at
all. Among records that could be examined, **9.1%** appear to be redundant. Across the whole corpus
the figure is 2.7%, but that denominator mixes "not redundant" with "never examined" — the true
rate is somewhere between, and closer to the higher figure if missingness is unrelated to
duplication.

**These reports have not been removed.** Dropping half a million records would move every number in
this document, and a matcher with unknown precision should not do that silently. The estimate is
published; removal is a parameter (`duplicates.remove`) that is off by default. Anyone who wants
the deduplicated variant can turn it on and re-run.

## Limitations

- **No causality, and no rates.** These are reporting associations. The denominator — people exposed
  without an event — is unknown, so incidence cannot be estimated.
- **MedDRA drift.** Preferred terms are treated as stable strings across 22 years, but MedDRA has
  been revised continuously and terms are renamed, split and merged. Some apparent temporal trend is
  vocabulary change. Correcting this needs the licensed MedDRA release, which cannot ship here.
- **No drug class hierarchy.** RxNorm→ATC mapping requires a UMLS licence. Cluster interpretation by
  pharmacologic class is therefore not attempted rather than approximated badly.
- **A substantial minority of "ingredients" are really product names.** See the section below —
  this is the most consequential limitation of the analysis and it is quantified there rather than
  summarized here.
- **Approximate q-values.** Benjamini–Hochberg assumes independence or positive dependence. Drug–event
  tests share reports and are dependent, so the stated FDR level is approximate.
- **Independent duplicates are estimated, not removed.** See the section above: ~9% of linkable
  records. Blocking makes that a lower bound, and the reports remain in every table here.
- **Partial dates.** Dates given as year or year-month are completed to the start of the period,
  biasing those toward January and month-starts.

## Reproducing this

```bash
git clone https://github.com/neksa/openfda-faers && cd openfda-faers
uv sync
dvc repro
```

No credentials and no cloud storage are needed: FDA hosts the source archives, `dvc.lock` pins every
intermediate by hash, and `results/source_manifest.json` records a SHA-256 for each of the
`{python} n(manifest["n_quarters"])` inputs. FDA republishes quarterly extracts as snapshots and can
revise them in place, so `faers verify` reports drift against those checksums rather than letting it
change the numbers silently.

Full run is roughly 25 minutes on a 10-core laptop, dominated by the download.
